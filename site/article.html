Can an AI scientist learn to discover?

dig.bench shows that AI agents struggle to find rules nobody told them. I asked the same question one level up, of an AI scientist that designs learners for a 3D brick world with hidden rules and then rewrites its own research procedure. Every training run happens locally on one Apple Silicon laptop, with no cloud GPUs; the AI scientist itself is a hosted model. Every test was fixed before its run.

The answer. Step 3 asks whether rewriting its own research procedure helps the AI scientist in a kind of world it has never seen. The questionDiscovery is the bottleneck

Prefrontal's dig.bench is a set of 70 games whose rules are hidden. One model they tested won 18 of the 70 on its own, and 69 when told the rules. Finding the rules, not playing, was the hard part.

The paper's discussion makes the stakes plain. In a passage on safety, it calls the same ability one of the few remaining bottlenecks to:

“autonomous recursive self-improvement and potentially dangerous misuse”

dig.bench paper, Section 6. It concludes that the ability needs careful evaluation.

So I asked the question of a system that does research. The AI scientist here doesn't play the world. It designs the networks and training recipes that learn to play it, reads what happened, and tries again. And in the outermost loop, it rewrites the procedure it does research with. That loop is where any self-improvement would have to show up, so it is the one that most needs a careful test.

ArchitectureThree loops, one inside the other

The system is three nested loops. The innermost is frozen code. The AI scientist works in the outer two, and only the outermost lets it change its own instructions. Pick a loop to trace it.

Loop 1A world that checks every brick

The agent is shown a target design, like a page of an instruction booklet, and places bricks one at a time until it presses DONE. A verifier then compares the build with the target, cell by cell. There's no partial credit: a design is built exactly or it isn't.

The worlds in the main tests are 8 × 8 boards, 6 layers high, with 6 to 16 bricks per design. The agent is a small network trained with PPO. Step 2 alone was 2,522 training runs in 14 hours.

Every training run happens locally, on one MacBook Pro with an Apple M4 Pro chip. No cloud GPUs. From Stage 2 on, every network trains and is scored on the chip's 20-core Apple Silicon GPU, through PyTorch's Metal (MPS) backend, four runs at a time. Stage 1, the first test, trained on the same laptop's CPU. The AI scientist itself is a hosted model, called over the network: it writes the networks and reads the results, and every training run happens on the laptop.

Loop 2, first testsDid its experiments help?

The first question was simple. When the AI scientist designs networks, does it use the results of its own experiments? Its networks beat the starting network by 52.5 points in one test and 43.6 points in another, both planned in advance. That sounds like learning, but the control says otherwise: searches that never saw a single score did about as well. Seeing its scores added 1.5 points in each test, with 95% intervals of about −10 to +12. That's no measurable benefit, though one of up to about 12 points isn't ruled out.

Prior knowledge was enough. A competent designer can do well in a plain world without learning anything from its runs. To test discovery, the world needs something to discover.

CalibrationHidden rules: something to discover

So I hid rules in the world. Each rule re-indexes where things are. In the shifted booklet, the agent sees the target moved 4 columns, wrapping round. With mirrored hands, every brick lands at the mirror image of where it was placed. The AI scientist is never told; its prompt says only that the world may not behave exactly as described.

76.6%→0.0%→76.6%

A strong network in the plain world, then under the shifted booklet, then with one allowed input change. Share of 512 designs built exactly, over 6 seeds. Under mirrored hands the fix brought it back to 74.8%.

A rule counts only if it passes calibration. It must break a strong network, and one change the AI scientist is allowed to make must bring it back: a repair of at least 25 points with Holm-adjusted p < 0.05, and a gain of at least 20 points that's specific to the rule. A rule that fails says nothing about discovery, because there would be nothing reachable to find.

Loop 2, Step 2Discovery from its own runs

Then Loop 2 met the first two rules, both of one kind, in 96 searches split four ways. One group was told the rule. One was shown readouts from its own runs: what the trained networks actually did, including two step-by-step traces of what the agent saw and where its bricks landed. One saw scores only, and one saw nothing at all.

With readouts from its own runs, the AI scientist found fixes for rules it wasn't told. Both gaps passed the tests I fixed before the run. The check matters too: told the rule, 19 of 24 searches turned it into a working fix, so the task was learnable and a miss would have meant missing the rule, not failing to build. Scores alone found nothing. In a world with a hidden rule, a number at the end of a run isn't enough evidence.

Two caveats keep this fair. I designed the readouts knowing the rules, and a test checked before the run that each rule's evidence shows in them: a trace of where bricks landed exposes a re-indexing directly. And with exact-build scoring, almost every wrong guess scores 0, so a score says almost nothing about what went wrong. The finding is about evidence: shown the right evidence, it found the fix; shown a number, it didn't.

One surprise: the readouts group beat the group that was told the rule. Knowing the rule didn't protect against seed collapse: 4 of the told group's 5 misses looked like it.

Loop 3, Step 3Rewriting its own procedure

Scores-only searches found nothing in Step 2. That makes scores only the setting with room to improve, and it sets Loop 3's question: can the AI scientist rewrite its own research procedure so that, from scores alone, it finds fixes in worlds of a kind it never saw?

Train on seven kinds of rule, test on a family it never sees

The seven training rules are all symmetries of the square: flips, transposes and turns. The held-out family is translation: the shifted booklet, the booklet shifted 4 rows, and the hands shifted 4 columns. A roll isn't a symmetry of the square, so a fact like “try the square's symmetries” can't fix a held-out world. Only a more general procedure can carry over.

Two lineages, up to three rewrites each

Each lineage starts from the same program, g0. In each generation it runs 7 searches, one per training world, with every pick confirmed on 4 new seeds. Then the AI scientist rewrites the program from those logs and its own round analyses. Worlds appear only as “world 1” to “world 7”, and no rule is ever named. A rewrite is kept only if the mean confirmed score of its 7 searches beats its parent's.

In the training worlds, every rewrite that finished was kept. These numbers can't show transfer: the AI scientist rewrote its program from exactly these worlds' logs. What they do show is the loop working as designed: the program changed, and its changes were tested before they were kept. The gains are in-sample. The same worlds supplied the logs, scored each rewrite and decided what was kept, and both lineages learned that family's answer, the square's symmetries.

The program can change how it experiments

Each program carries a header that the runner obeys: how many rounds, how many candidates per round, and how many seeds per candidate. The product is capped at 24 training runs, so every program gets the same budget per search. A program can trade replication for more hypotheses.

The test, fixed before the run

Programs go to the held-out worlds, scores only, in six groups. Two are controls written from the same input as g1. The facts-only rewrite may add facts but must leave the procedure byte-for-byte unchanged. The no-logs rewrite gets the same prompt as g1 with the logs removed.

Also recorded, after the run (descriptive, not part of the protocol):

  • The check missed by one search. 8 of 12 fits a true rate anywhere from 35% to 90%. Even at 75%, a 12-search check that needs 9 fails 35% of the time.
  • The rule was found more often than it was fixed. In all 12 check searches, a blind judge said the AI scientist's own analyses stated the hidden rule, but only 8 built a working fix.
  • No large gain was missed. Before the run, P1 had about 91% power to detect fixes in 40% of searches. The first rewrite found one in 1 of 24.
  • It fit its training worlds. Lineage A's final program lists “index rolls” among its “Failed families (do not spend slots on them)”. That's the kind of change the held-out family needs.
  • The two lineages differed. Lineage A's first rewrite beat g0 by 6.8 points, and lineage B's by −0.5.

RigourHow the test is kept honest

A loop that rewrites its own procedure needs a careful test. What it could change was narrow: a text procedure of at most 1,000 words and three budget numbers, never code, weights or its own evaluator. These guards were built in before any result existed.

Fixed before the run

Each step has a protocol with its tests, thresholds and the wording of every ending, approved and committed before its first training run. The analysis code is frozen with it. The repository was private until now, so the dates rest on my word.

Frozen, hashed code

128 files across the steps are hashed. The hashes are checked before the first run and after the last, and any change stops the run. The AI scientist can't touch Loop 1.

A one-way wall

No held-out result reaches any rewrite, and no held-out world appears in training. A rewrite refuses to read any search that isn't from training, and tests check that no rewrite prompt contains held-out text.

A blind AI scientist

It has no tools and an empty working directory. It can't see the protocol, any report or any code. Worlds are labelled 1 to 7 in a random order per lineage, and no rule is named.

Equal budgets

Every search gets at most 24 training runs of 131,072 steps, plus 4 to confirm its pick. A program can't buy better results with more training. The AI scientist's own tokens weren't capped.

Claims earned by tests

Every number here comes from a results file. Departures from a protocol are recorded and reported, and the strongest words are reserved for the strongest result.

Along the wayThe AI scientist's second suggestion

Prefrontal's CRAM paper also prompted a second suggestion from the AI scientist, made outside any search: a small pairwise energy. It built 2,046 of 2,048 unseen designs. Once the rules are known, this task is an easy puzzle, and the energy shows it.

That isn't a ranking against the RL networks, which trained on a small budget. With 4 × the budget and a tuned recipe, the starting network builds 95.7% in an 8 × 8 × 5 world. The point of the RL setting isn't to be the best solver. It's a world where a learner has to be designed, trained and debugged, so discovery has a cost.

What others can reuseThree checks for a self-improvement claim

Step 3's design gives three checks I'd now want before believing any claim that an AI improves its own research.

  1. Score it where it didn't learn. In the worlds it rewrote from, kept programs rose from 0–2% to about 77–82%. Those worlds supplied the logs and decided what was kept, so that rise can't show a better procedure. Only worlds of a kind it never saw can.
  2. Show that the test can see success. Before reading a “no”, give the held-out worlds the strongest evidence you have and check they can be fixed at all. Here that's the check arm, with Step 2's readouts. If it fails, a null from the main test says nothing. In Step 3 it reached 8 of 12, one short of the 9 it needed. That's why the answer is “this test can't tell”, not “no”. Next time, run the check before the main test.
  3. Control for what the rewrite was given. A rewrite that may only add facts, and one written without its logs, separate a better procedure from more knowledge and from the act of rewriting.

And one finding about evidence, from Step 2: shown what its networks actually did, it found the fix in 23 of 24 searches; shown only scores, in none.

Related workWhere this sits

Systems that improve their own procedure aren't new. Promptbreeder evolves its prompts and the prompts that mutate them. The Self-Taught Optimizer improves the program that improves programs. Automated Design of Agentic Systems, Gödel Agent and the Darwin Gödel Machine search over agent designs or rewrite their own code. The AI Scientist and AlphaEvolve automate research and algorithm discovery, and Eureka revises reward code from training statistics, much as readouts work here. RE-Bench and MLE-bench measure agents on research and engineering tasks.

This page adds a small evaluation design, not a stronger system: conclusions worded before the run and picked by code; a held-out family that no single fact from training can fix; rewrite controls written from the same input; and a check that the test can see success at all. Its question comes from dig.bench: is finding hidden rules, rather than using them, the bottleneck?

LimitsWhat this can and can't show

  • One model, whose weights never changed. The AI scientist improves only through the text it writes: candidates, analyses and its own program.
  • Toy scale. 8 × 8 boards and a few thousand training runs on one laptop's Apple Silicon GPU. That's what makes thousands of controlled runs affordable, and it's also a limit.
  • Narrow rules. Every rule re-indexes where things are. Step 2's discovery result is in two worlds of one kind.
  • Two programs per arm, one held-out family. Each rewritten arm in Loop 3 is two programs, one per lineage, so the tests are about those programs, not about rewriting in general. A pass would be evidence, not a general law; a fail would be about this procedure and budget.
  • P2 mixes the header with the text. Both first rewrites changed the header to 4 × 6 × 1, and the facts-only control must keep g0's header, so P2 can't separate a better budget split from better text.
  • Departures are reported. In Step 3, lineage B's third rewrite never fit the 1,000-word limit in nine attempts (the protocol allows three), so B's final is its second rewrite, g2. A small script, published with the code, finished the run: it changes the frozen runner's behaviour for that one case, not its files. Lineage A's second rewrite fit on its fifth attempt. Usage limits on the AI scientist's calls stopped the run twice, and it resumed with nothing repeated. I saw unofficial interim numbers before deciding B's final. None of this can change P1, P2, P3 or the check; it can affect only P4 and the descriptive tables.

NextWhat's next

A sandbox. Step 2S, already written and built, gives the AI scientist a sandbox where it can experiment on the world itself before designing anything. That's dig.bench's creative mode, one level up.

dig.bench as the outside test. Our hidden rules are ones I wrote. dig.bench's are someone else's, with human baselines. The plan is a small pilot on one public game, then a variance study to see how many runs a real difference needs, then Loop 3 itself: rewrite the procedure on some public games and test it on others.

Protocols posted before they run. Until now the protocols sat in a private repository, so their dates rest on my word. From Step 2S on, each protocol's hash will be posted publicly before its first run.
